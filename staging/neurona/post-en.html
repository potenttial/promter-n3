The math: why a “thank you” costs US$5.95 a year

An AI has no memory between messages. Every time you write something, the system sends it the whole conversation again: your instructions, the documents you pasted and every previous answer. Those are input tokens. Then the model writes its reply, the output tokens, which cost several times more.

That's why the words “thank you” barely weigh anything: one or two tokens. What weighs is the reread. A “thank you” at the end of a long chat costs almost as much as a new question.

In a company that connects AI to its systems, it does show up on the bill: 1,400 employees saying thank you once a day, Monday to Friday, in a work chat add up to about US$5,900 a year.

How much do companies spend on AI per employee?

In the United States, the average company spends US$2,068 a year per employee on AI in 2026, 50% more than in 2025, according to Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta data compiled by Rize. The average hides a wide spread: the median company spends less than 200 dollars, and the top 10% at least 2,800.

Standard commercial licenses such as Microsoft Copilot, Claude Team or Cursor cost 20 to 30 dollars a month per user: US$240 to US$360 a year. At API prices, a single daily “thank you” equals almost 2% of what a 20-dollar license costs. That share doesn't show up on the bill, but it uses up the allowance the company already paid for.

What Sam Altman said

In April 2025, someone on X asked OpenAI's CEO how much money the company loses in electricity because people type “please” and “thank you”. He answered: “tens of millions of dollars well spent—you never know”. And it isn't an isolated case: nearly 70% of people who use artificial intelligence (such as ChatGPT or Gemini) tend to be polite: they greet it, say please or say thank you, according to El Confidencial (February 2025, in Spanish). A Future survey from the same year puts it at 67% of users in the United States.

I'm not writing this so you stop saying thank you. I'm writing it because that figure shows something more important: millions of people use AI without knowing what each message consumes. That has a name.

The problem isn't politeness, it's indifference

In my article Indifference is your brand's number one enemy (in Spanish) I explain that indifference is the absence of attention and attachment. With AI, it shows up in habits nobody measures:

  • The repetitive: one chat for everything for weeks. Every new question drags along thousands of tokens of history that no longer matters.
  • The superficial: “make me something nice”. With no goal or format, the first answer misses and has to be requested three times.
  • The undefined: drip-feeding the idea: “hi”, “you there?”, “let me explain”. Every message bills everything before it again.
  • Generalizing: using the most expensive model to fix spelling, because “all AIs are the same”.

The antidote is the same one I propose for brands: Curioxoma, professionalized curiosity. Asking yourself what you are requesting, why, and what it costs to request it is curiosity applied to how you use technology.

A vague prompt produces slop

In Digital entropy: slop and enshittification (in Spanish) I describe how synthetic noise began to drown out the human signal. Slop isn't born only in content farms. It's also born at the office, every time someone asks AI for a text without saying who it's for, what it's for or how long it should be.

The result is workslop: long answers that have to be read, fixed and requested again. You pay twice: in tokens and in human attention. The indifference behind the prompt turns into entropy inside the company.

AI is not for paying less attention

There's a dangerous idea going around: that AI is for thinking less. It's the opposite. AI didn't arrive so we could pay less attention, but so we could be more selective about investing it. You delegate the repetitive work to focus your quality attention on what only you can decide.

The first principle of Thinktech (in Spanish) says your attention is your energy. Tokens are the AI's energy. A good prompt is where both energies meet without waste: your attention decides what to ask, and the AI spends only what it needs to solve it.

That's why I insist that thinking is technology. Our greatest contribution in the age of AI isn't writing faster or producing more content. It's knowing how to think: being clear about the goal, the context and the criteria before asking. Whoever thinks well writes better prompts, and whoever writes better prompts spends less.

How to optimize tokens with technological awareness

A Worklytics analysis published in 2026 estimates that asking for the exact format and length cuts each task's tokens by 45% to 80%: up to 80% on meeting notes, 60–70% on summaries and 50–65% on emails. Five habits to get there:

  1. One complete message. Context, task, format and limits together. No “I'll send more info in a moment”.
  2. Set a cap. “100 words max” or “5 bullets”. Output is the most expensive part.
  3. One chat per topic. Start a new one when you change subjects.
  4. The right-sized model. A light one to summarize or translate; the expensive one to analyze and decide.
  5. Say thanks inside your next prompt. “Thanks, now turn it into a table” costs the same as the request alone and keeps the courtesy.

Why does a short message cost so much?

Because the AI rereads the whole conversation before answering. The cost depends on the history, not on the length of your message.

Should I stop being polite to AI?

No. Say thanks inside your next useful prompt, for example: “Thanks, now summarize it in 5 bullets”. You keep the courtesy and don't pay for an extra reread.
